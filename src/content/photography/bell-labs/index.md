The Bell Labs photo section.

I spent eleven and a half years in corporate innovation, which meant getting paid to help other people have ideas. Three-letter agencies. Defense contractors. A company that made very expensive shoes. Different budgets, same problem: everybody wants the breakthrough, nobody wants the conditions that produce one. Which is how I ended up here, standing in a New Jersey parking lot photographing a building.

Bell Labs is where the twentieth century actually got built. The transistor came out of the Murray Hill campus in 1947, and everything you are holding, watching, or arguing on descends from it. But the reason has less to do with genius than with floor plans. Mervin Kelly ran the place, and his real invention was an arrangement of hallways. He put physicists next to metallurgists next to mechanical engineers and made the corridors long enough that you could not walk to lunch without colliding with someone whose work you did not understand. That was the point. His other insight, the one nobody has successfully copied since, was that anyone could talk to anyone. You could knock on a Nobel laureate's door and he was expected to answer.

Holmdel is that idea rendered in mirrored glass. Eero Saarinen designed it, it opened in 1962, and it looks less like a laboratory than like something that landed. A couple of miles from here, two radio astronomers pointed a horn antenna at the sky, could not get rid of a faint hiss, checked for loose wiring and pigeon droppings, and accidentally found the leftover heat of the Big Bang. It is a Verizon-adjacent office park with a coffee shop now. I have sat in a lot of innovation workshops. None of them were that.
