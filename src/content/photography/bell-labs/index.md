I spent eleven and a half years in corporate innovation, which meant getting paid to help other people have ideas. Three-letter agencies. Defense contractors. A company that made very expensive shoes. Different budgets, same problem: everybody wants the breakthrough, nobody wants the conditions that produce one. Which is how I ended up here, standing in a New Jersey parking lot photographing a building.

I grew up in Florida making fun of New Jersey. Now I live in New Jersey and we make fun of Florida. Everybody seems content with the arrangement. What gets left out is that New Jersey built most of the modern world. I live in Bloomfield, about four miles from Thomas Edison's laboratory and about thirty from Bell Labs. Do the math on that. You can drive from one to the other on a Saturday afternoon, and somewhere in that errand is recorded sound, the light bulb, the transistor, and the operating system your phone is running on.

Bell Labs is where the twentieth century actually got built. The transistor came out of the Murray Hill campus in 1947, and everything you're holding, watching, or arguing on descends from it. But the reason has less to do with genius than with floor plans. Mervin Kelly ran the place, and his real invention was an arrangement of hallways. He put physicists next to metallurgists next to mechanical engineers and made the corridors long enough that you couldn't walk to lunch without colliding with someone whose work you didn't understand. That was the point. His other insight, the one nobody has successfully copied since, was that anyone could talk to anyone. You could knock on a Nobel laureate's door and he was expected to answer.

Holmdel is that idea rendered in mirrored glass. Eero Saarinen designed it, it opened in 1962, and it looks less like a laboratory than like something that landed. A couple of miles from here, two radio astronomers pointed a horn antenna at the sky, couldn't get rid of a faint hiss, checked for loose wiring and pigeon droppings, and accidentally found the leftover heat of the Big Bang. It's a Verizon-adjacent office park with a coffee shop now. I've sat in a lot of innovation workshops. None of them were that.

Maybe the most important set of buildings of the twentieth century. There's nothing close, to be honest with you.
